# 15 — THE LONG LIGHT

The plate had a beam of light lying across it.

It came in at the top left corner, went down through the middle in a long slow curve, and went out at the bottom right. Ruled straight across the middle, it missed the line by a quarter turn.

It did not touch anything on the way. It came off the edge and went to the edge.

Behind it was a great ring of pale blue line — circles inside circles, divided at intervals, ticked round the outside, and four small rectangles at the four points of a cross, a circle in each.

Inside those four shapes were more marks, cut in the same line, each one a stack of nine short bars.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

A diagonal of light crossing the frame makes it alive, they wrote. That is the whole of why they had kept looking at it.

Then they wrote, lower down: it enters and it leaves.

They went out into the yard at four in the afternoon, the one time of day they could have said was wrong.

There was a beam in the yard. There had been a beam in the yard every afternoon for eleven years.

It came over the north wall and crossed the flagstones and finished at the drainpipe in a clean edge, and past the drainpipe there was no beam anywhere.

The Reader stood in it. It was warm and it had a shape and it had an end, and the end was a place.

Then they stood with the plate in their hands and read it again, and the difference took a while to arrive because it was such a small difference.

The plate's beam had no end in it.

Nothing took it. No wall, no bench, no stone, no edge. It went off the side of the picture with the brightness it came on with.

So either it was not light, or it was light going on being light in a place with nothing in it to take the light.

The Reader went back in and read the blue behind it, which they had not done, because a beautiful background is not the sort of thing a person reads.

The circles were not a setting. They were a dial.

Concentric, divided at even intervals, ticked at the outside edge, four rectangles at the four points. That is not decoration. It is an instrument for telling where a thing is by laying it against a mark that does not move.

And then the finding, which took the rest of the afternoon.

A light that stops has been taken by something. A light that does not stop has been taken by nothing at all.

So the beam was not in a room. It was not going anywhere. It was a line.

And a line across a dial is not light. It is the datum — put in first so that everything else has something to be measured from.

It was not lighting the field. It was measuring it.

The Reader wrote that down and left it a long time, because it changes what the plate is, and nothing else on it can then be read the same way.

But there was one thing on the plate left that they could still read, which was what was inside the four rectangles.

Nine short bars, stacked, in each one. Four of them, and the four were the same.

That is the tower.

The one in the field. Nine courses and a stub of arch on the east side and the stones running out from its foot in a fan.

A dial needs four marks, because four points is how a dial knows which way it is facing. North, east, south, west, and every one of the four on this plate was the tower.

So the tower was not in the picture. The tower was north.

The cross and the compass are the same mark, and somebody had put the tower at both ends of it.

The Reader sat with the plate flat in front of them and thought about the voice in the glyph, which was what the mark had been for six months and they had never had a noun for.

Every glyph in the drawer had been an arrangement. An arrangement stands for a relation between things. This one said a thing that had stood in a field they had walked past two hundred times.

The glyph was not writing. It was a tower, standing at the four points of the dial since the first plate, holding the orientation of the whole instrument.

So the beam was pointed at the fourth mark. East.

They went out to check it and they took the ruler, which was the first thing they had done for a plate in a fortnight.

The beam in the yard came over the wall and crossed and did what it always did, and where it always finished, at the drainpipe, the ground changed.

It went on.

Past the drainpipe, under the gate, and out into the field on the other side, in one straight run, the whole length of the yard, because there was nothing east of the gate to stop it.

The Reader had lived here eleven years and had never once measured the bottom of the gate.

They got the ruler down there in the dark and it was four inches.

Four inches of open air between the bottom rail of the gate and the flagstones, along its whole length, and nothing in it but shadow and a leaf at each end.

So the gate had never once been shut on the ground. Not in eleven years and not before, because the hinges hold it four inches up and there is nothing down there to have been raised off.

The Reader put both hands under the bottom rail and pushed, and the gate came up four inches on its own and would not go back.

Not an open gate. A raised one. Something that looks shut and is not shut and never was.

They stood in the dark yard with the ruler in their hand and the beam going east under the gate, and understood what the plate was.

Four inches. That is the width of the beam.

The beam was not measuring the field. It was measuring the gap, and the only thing in this house that stands on nothing is the gate.

The beam running east under the gate, the gate standing on nothing.
