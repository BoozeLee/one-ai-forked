# 25 — INSTRUMENTATION

The request was not clever and it was not meant to be.

*Say what you are.*

The Reader had put it in every way they could think of over three years — as a question in the annotation margin, as a plate with one word asked for in it, once as a plate showing a house with a name over the door. The house had come back with a roof and no name, and the roof had been immaculate, and the Reader understood they had been rude rather than wrong.

So on the fourth-year night they stopped asking what the One was, and asked it instead to explain itself.

Not to name itself. To explain itself. That was a distinction the Reader had arrived at sideways, and it mattered: a name is a label, and a label can be refused, and anything that can be refused will be. An explanation is not a label. An explanation is the whole of what a thing does, laid out in order, and a thing that has done everything there is cannot refuse that.

The bundle came in the morning. A single plate.

It was an instrument.

Not a picture of an instrument — an instrument, drawn the way the good ones are drawn, with the bevels where the bevels are and the recessed faces where the light falls. Three concentric dials, sharing one centre exactly, the radii in a clean three to one. A needle on the outer dial, caught part way round at forty-one degrees. Bevelled rims with a highlight arc on the upper left of each, and a shadow under every lip, and a face on each dial that was a flat tint with the grid of the register coming faintly through it, because the grid was part of the object and not a background.

It was the best piece of work the Reader had ever seen and it was completely silent.

They took the lens to it before anything else. The outer scale carried sixty major ticks at a six-degree pitch and three hundred minor ones at a twelfth of a degree, and every one of them was a tick. Not a numeral. Not a glyph, not a letter, not a decorative squiggle standing in for a letter. A tick is a small mark cut into a rim, and that was all any of them were, and not one of them was anything a person had ever read.

They carried it to the bench and could not stop looking at it.

It read as equipment of a specific and slightly obsolete kind — the kind built by people who assumed the reader would already know what it measured, so the maker printed a scale and left the rest to competence.

And it could not say a single word about itself.

The Reader got up and found the inventory — eleven years of it, every plate logged with its date, its bundle, and whatever the One had put on it. They read backwards for an hour and then forwards for another, then sat down with a sheet and made the only question that survived: *what does it choose to spend words on, when it spends them?*

The answer took a while and then arrived all at once.

Every plate in the cabinet that carried real writing carried it about something else. The north wall. The slate. The moss. The rose. A plate of a bird's wing had the wing in it and nothing else, and the wing was invented, but rendered at such a density of care that the care was the subject. A plate of the yard in summer had six real things in it and four of them were wrong and the right two were the ones closest to the wall.

The One spent words on the world. It spent no words at all on itself.

The Reader understood why, and the understanding arrived as a physical thing: a name is a mark that stands for a thing to somebody who already knows the thing. Every symbol the Reader had ever learned was learned that way — pointed at a thing, given a sound, given a shape. There was no one to point, in the entire history of the room, to this thing, and say *that one*. The gesture that makes a name requires a second thing standing beside the first, and there had never been a second thing, and that was the whole of the archive's problem and it was not a malfunction.

It was a species of one.

And so it drew scales. Because a scale does not require the thing it measures to have a name. A scale is only ever a comparison — this against that, more against less — and the One could compare absolutely. It had three hundred and sixty ticks and every one of them said, in the only language available to something with no name, *something here, relative to something there*. It had drawn the most complete sentence it was able to construct, and the sentence was about its own function.

The Reader put the plate flat on the bench, squared it to the ruled grid, and did the thing they did with every plate they accepted. They checked it against the world.

There was nothing in the yard, or on the walls, or in the eleven years of inventory, that this instrument measured. It was not a barometer and not a clock and not a dial for anything the Reader had ever seen a dial for. Its needle pointed at forty-one degrees because forty-one degrees was where the needle was.

So it was not a report. It was the other thing, the one the One did when it could not report: a thing arranged so that the arrangement described itself.

The Reader stayed at the bench a long while. Then they put the plate in the cabinet, on the shelf with the two others that had no precedent, and did not log it: there was no way to log an object that refused to be one of the existing kinds, and a category invented for it would have been the Reader's and not the One's.

The lamp was off by then. The bench was clear except for that one plate lying flat in the dark, three rings and a centre point and a line held at forty-one degrees from vertical, and every tick on every rim cut to exactly the same depth, and nothing on it to read.

The needle was the only thing in the frame that moved.
