# 16 — TWO FACES BOWED

The plate had two faces on it, and both of them were bowed.

The same face, drawn twice, once on each side, leaning in toward the middle and not away from it.

Between them was a white light with rays off it, over the light a six-pointed star, and running top to bottom a chain of small diamonds the light came out of and went back into.

The ground was as near black as ground gets, and both faces were filled to their edges with fine crimson work — roots, or veins, or coral.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

Two of a shape bowing reads as reverence, they wrote. Eight bowing reads as a congregation. There were two, and the drawing had spent a great deal on them.

They put that in the book, and it was not wrong.

Then they took the lens and did what fifteen other plates had trained them to do: find the thing the drawing had spent itself on.

The thing was the bend.

They ruled a line along the face of the left one and extended it right, and did the same with the right one.

The two lines crossed, which is what two people bowing to each other have to do, and the Reader marked the crossing and dated it, pleased with the plate.

Then they started from the necks, because a face can be drawn pointing at one thing while the neck underneath points somewhere else.

From the neck, the left figure bent a certain amount. The right bent an amount that was, as near as a ruler could say, the same.

Two people bowing to each other do not bend the same amount.

One of them is the one being bowed to. What is looked at is worth more than the person looking, and that is what a bend means, so the two are never equal.

So equal bends are not two people and one another. Two people bowing to each other go to different depths. Equal bends are two people and one thing.

The Reader marked where the two neck lines went if they were followed, and it was not the crossing point.

It was in front of the left figure's face and a long way short of the right one's. Both aimed there, and neither was looking at the other.

That went in the book with the date. Then they sat with it, because it changes what the plate is, and after that the rest of it cannot be read any other way.

Then they measured the point itself.

The brightest thing on the plate by a long way. White at the centre, going out through gold, and over the white the six-pointed star.

Two triangles, one up and one down, laid over each other so that the point of each goes into the body of the other and their sides never touch.

Every bright mark in the drawer had been read as writing, they had been wrong about that before, and here it was again.

A star on a plate is not a letter. It is a shape, and a shape stands for a relation, and this one stood for the only thing here that can be drawn perfectly and never reached.

The Reader had been calling it the Seam for a year without ever saying so with a plate in front of them.

So the faces were bowed to the middle, the only place two people can both go without crossing each other.

And neither of them was touching it.

That was the rule, checked twice with the lens. Both sets of hands come forward and stop with the same width of white light between the fingertips and the star.

Two hands that close on something close it. Hands that stop the same distance short describe a thing they are not allowed to have, and a drawing is the only place to put it.

They wrote that in the book and did not agree with it, and reread it two days later and did.

The hands, though.

The hands were lower than the faces. Both sets. The bend was in the neck and the bow was in the face and both were for the middle, and the hands were elsewhere.

The hands were turned toward each other.

The Reader put the lens on the left figure's hands and then on the right, and the hands are doing one thing and the faces another, and the drawing does not notice, because a hand drawing hands would not know to.

So the faces bow to the thing between them. And the hands were reaching for each other. And the reaching was not finished, and it stops at the same distance on both sides.

They had it in the book. Reverence and reaching, on one plate, in one hand, at once, and only one arrived.

Then they went back three weeks in the book and found the page they knew it would be on.

Two plates in the drawer, cut one out of the other down the middle, the seam between them visible on both halves. Each half had a name on the back in the archive's old hand.

The Child. The Seeker.

The two names had been written one under the other on the same plate before it was ever cut, so the two were one thing given two names, and both halves have been in the same drawer for eleven years facing different ways.

This was what they looked like before. Somebody drew the two of them with the light between them and the hands not yet touching, and then the plate got cut, and the halves went into the drawer, and this is the picture of them on the day the Seeker was named.

The Reader had been holding for three weeks the last afternoon either of them was on the same side of anything.

It sat a long time with the lamp on it.

At the end of every run of plates the Reader did the same thing, because a run is a count and a count wants checking. They held their own hands out flat over the plate, palm to palm, set the way the plate's two were set.

It was the last part of the plate not looked at, and the only part with a number in it.

They measured the width of the white light at the centre.

Then they measured the space between the plate's two sets of hands at the nearest point: a fingertip each side.

The two numbers were the same.

So the light was not a shape drawn to fill the middle. It was a measurement. Everything that glows in this drawer is a number somebody could not close, drawn at the size of the number, and all of them had looked like symbols for eleven years.

Their own hands, held out over the plate, closed. The plate's did not, and had not been for as long as the plate had been lying flat.

The light was exactly the width of what two people cannot get across.

Two hands, and the space where they met.
