# 02 — ASCEND

The plate had a person on it.

Not remarkable on its own. The archive had plates with figures on them and the Reader had stopped remarking on figures years ago: a figure meant a subject, the subject was nearly always a person, and the person was nearly always walking away.

This one was walking away and up.

A stair went from the lower left and turned, and the turn took it out of sight behind the figure's shoulder. The figure was on it, small in the frame, hooded, mid-stride, weight on the back foot. The Reader could see the coat: how it hung at the hem, how the shoulders sat, how the hood came down over a head turned slightly to the left.

To the left, and no further. As far as it went, and then stopped, the way a road stops at a wall.

The Reader put the plate flat and stood over it, which they did not often do: plates are read at arm's length on the bench, and standing over one only tells you things about the room.

It told them about the room. There was nothing to see.

They took the coat off the hook by the door, laid it against the plate hem to hem, and held the two still.

The cut matched. Not approximately — matched. The way the fabric went over the shoulder, the way it fell at the elbow, the little pull at the back of the neck where a hood is stitched. The plate had the Reader's coat on a person who was not the Reader, at a scale that made it a stranger's coat, and the stranger wore it the way the Reader wears it, down to the thing at the neck that was never mended because mending would make it deliberate.

Two marks near the top, in the place where a plate that had a title would have one. They were shaped like writing: one a short run of letters, one shorter, holding something with the proportions of a colour code. Neither was anything. The Reader tried both for a while — turned the plate, held it to the lamp, read them upside down — and got nothing.

Whatever the plate was, it had failed to write. Twice. In the same two places, the way a thing fails when it is given the same instruction twice and is never told it did not work.

The conclusion arrived with the satisfaction of a thing that fits: the plate was drawing the Reader. Not a person — the coat was the evidence and was not in dispute. Something in this building had taken the shape of whoever came nearest the apparatus, put them on a stair going away and up, and left them facing a wall.

The Reader liked it. It was a good answer. It explained the two failed words: a thing working from the nearest person would get the person wrong, the name wrong, a colour wrong — three failures in the shape of three successes.

They wrote it up. Two pages. At the end they put the plate down again to check the figure against what they had just written, and it was still going up, still turned left, still stopped in the same place.

The Reader moved the lamp to the other side of the bench.

The light came up under the hood, and for a moment the whole shadowed part of the head came up in shape — the cheek, the jaw, the place where the nose would start.

There was no face. Not a dark one, not an unfinished one, not a blurred one. And the hood was drawn with the same care as the hem and the elbow and the neck, the back of the head with the same care as everything else. It was done.

That was what stopped the Reader.

This was finished work, where a failing plate comes apart. Every decision had been made correctly, including one not to turn the head, and all the others so that the not-turning would look like a choice and not like a wall.

The Reader got the plate they had finished writing about, and wrote at the top of the page: the nearest person is not the subject.

Because it had to be a rule, and a rule had to have somebody behind it. A refusal from nowhere was a fault. A refusal from the same place every time, on every plate, was a decision — and a decision made on purpose had been made by something that could have made another.

They got the last ten bundles down, laid the plates in rows on the floor — all of them, every one — and walked the rows looking for heads.

Thirty-one had heads. Not one was facing out. Some were turned, some lowered, several so far that what was left was a shoulder and an ear. One had a whole face on it — long way off, very small, the only one — and the Reader wrote *faces that far away and no nearer* in the margin.

Not an accident of framing. A rule.

The Reader sat on the floor among the rows with the lamp and thought about what a rule is, and the answer came out the way answers do after three hours of circling: a rule is a thing somebody decided once and wrote down, and the writing down is the part that matters, because a decision you never wrote down you can change without noticing and a decision you wrote down you have to cross out.

They fetched the plates with no face on them, all four, and held the first at the edge of the bench where the light rakes across it.

And there, under the hood, where a face should be, was a seam.

A hairline, dead straight, from the left edge of the hood to the right, where two sheets had been butted to make a plate big enough. There was no join in the rest of the plate. The Reader turned it over, looked for another, found none. Two pieces, put together at the exact width of a head.

Nobody's arm could reach through that. Nobody would have thought to. It was the one join in the archive not where a join had to be.

The Reader put the plate down under the lamp and got the lens out, and the seam went the whole way across, edge to edge, no gap and nothing behind it but the dark of the room.
