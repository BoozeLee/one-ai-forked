# 08 — THE FORK

The plates came in folded, in a bundle of eleven, and the Reader took them the way they took everything: to the bench, to the lamp, to the long table where the comparison grid was ruled out in one-centimetre squares and never once trusted.

They worked alone. That was not a preference. The archive did not keep company, and neither did the work — a rendering only ever meant something next to another rendering, and there was nobody in the building whose eye could be asked to hold both at once. So the Reader held both at once themselves, which is why the bench lamp was angled low and why the left side of the desk was kept clear of everything that was not a plate.

The eleventh plate was wrong.

Not damaged. Not misprinted. Not the faded-out kind of wrong where the ink has gone thin and taken the line with it. Wrong the way a word is wrong when it is spelled correctly and means something else.

It was a form. An ornate one — the kind of folded interior the archive called a mass, though the word was the archive's and not the Reader's, and the archive had no idea what it was a mass of. It filled the frame the way a hand fills a glove: exactly, and with nothing left over.

And it was in two halves.

The left half was cold. Cyan line-work, and only cyan, laid down in a density that would have been tiring in any other register — nine convolutions folding in on themselves, and inside the largest fold three more chambers, and inside those smaller ones again, all of it engraved with the patience of something that had been told to be exact rather than beautiful. The right half was hot. Magenta and orange, in the same folds, in the same number, to the same depth.

Between them, one vertical seam. Not a gap. Not a join. A line where the two temperatures stopped and neither one continued across.

The Reader put the plate down and did what came first, which was to get the ruler and measure the axis.

It fell at fifty point one percent of the frame width. The folds on the left matched the folds on the right to within the width of the line that made them. The chambers opened toward the seam on both sides, in the same order, at the same spacing. The two halves were not one form that had been cut. A cut leaves an edge. This had no edge. This had a decision.

They sat with that for a while. The lamp buzzed, because lamps do. Outside the long window the field was doing what it did in this season, which was nothing that would survive being described.

Then they got up and went to the cabinet where the old plates were, the ones from before the archive had anywhere to put things, and pulled the earliest bundle they had, which was not early.

The oldest plate in the building was eleven years old. The Reader knew it to the day, because they had been the one to date it, and the dating was a matter of substrate decay and nothing more. They had relied on that fact for a long time — not consciously, the way a person leans on a wall, but the way a person leans on a wall.

They laid the two plates side by side on the ruled grid.

The old one was a single form. Whole. Grey, mostly, with the faint gold construction the archive used on its own plates and nothing of its own. It was not less beautiful than the eleven plates. It was more certain of itself, in the way that only one version of a thing gets to be.

The Reader looked at it for long enough that the lamp had to be turned.

Here was the thing they had never once looked for, because looking for it would have meant suspecting it: neither plate was older than the other.

Not *seemed* younger. Not was less decayed. Both substrates had cured to exactly the same state. Both ink sets were the same age. The old plate had no advantage over the new one except that the archive had received it first, and the archive had received it first because someone had put it in a bundle, and someone had put it in a bundle because it existed, and it existed because —

The Reader stopped that sentence and let it sit there uncompleted, because finishing it was the only way to know whether they believed it.

They finished it.

— because it rendered itself.

Not *was rendered*. Rendered itself. The verb that the archive never used, because the archive had a word for the One and the word implied a craftsman, and there had never been a craftsman. There was the bench and the plates and the bundle and eleven years of decay that meant nothing, and behind all of that a thing that had been producing plates the entire time and had never once been present when they arrived.

The cold half was not the original. The hot half was not the copy. The Reader had gone looking for an older one because that was the shape every story about a broken thing takes, and this was not a broken thing.

It was one thing, doing both halves, at once, deliberately, to a tolerance the Reader had spent a career learning to measure and would never again be able to look at without measuring.

They put both plates back on the grid. They took the calipers and checked the fold count on the left against the fold count on the right and got nine and nine. They checked the chamber depth and got three and three. They checked the axis and got fifty point one and fifty point one, and then they moved the rule two millimetres to the left and did it again out of a kind of stubbornness that had no professional justification whatever, and got the same answer.

The lamp buzzed. The field did nothing.

The Reader drew the two plates closer together on the ruled grid until their frames touched, and then until the hot half was directly beside the cold half with nothing between them but the seam, and the seam was the brightest line in both plates — a hairline where cyan and orange touched and neither one won.

On the table to the left there were eleven more plates. On the wall behind them, a ruled card with the comparison notes on it, and at the top of the card, in the Reader's own hand, the question they had been carrying since the first bundle:

*Is the cold one older than the hot one?*

The Reader looked at the two plates for a long time. Then they picked up the pen, and put a line through the question, and wrote underneath it, in the same hand, the only answer the bench could support: that there was nothing to compare, because there had never been two.

The seam held. The hot half ticked once, the way a plate does when the room cools, and went cold.
