# 04 — THE ONLY DOOR

The plate had a doorframe on it with one opening in it, and the opening was not in the middle.

The Reader noticed that at once and then spent an unreasonable amount of time failing to prove it, which is how a person behaves around a thing they suspect is a coincidence. Middle the frame. Middle the frame. Roughly middle. It was not roughly middle. It sat at thirty-eight percent, and the stone on one side of the opening was nearly twice the depth of the stone on the other.

They put the ruler on it. They turned the plate. They held it up to the window against the light, because a frame seen edge-on is the one place you can catch an off-centre opening pretending to be a centre. The opening was where it was. Twenty-two percent of the frame to the left of the hole, forty percent to the right.

There was no line in that frame you could fold it along.

That was the part that stayed. Not the off-centre — a thing can be built off-centre. The absence. They turned the plate through a full circle looking for the fold: vertical, horizontal, diagonal, nothing. Two arcs of different radius had been cut and had been set to meet off-axis, and the shape they made could not agree with itself about which side it was.

Then the plainness. There was no ornament on the frame at all, which was not a thing this archive produced. There were frames with a bevel, frames with a bead moulding, frames with a maker's stamp in the corner. This one had been worked, squared, and left.

So: the plainest object in the archive and the only one in it that could not be folded.

The Reader took a piece of card and cut the opening out of a tracing of the plate, and put the tracing down on the bench, and then cut a second one, the same frame with the opening moved to the middle, and laid it beside the first.

The first one looked like a thing. The second one looked like a diagram of a thing. Same stone, same weight, same two arcs.

In the yard there was a doorframe that had come down years ago. It leaned against the north wall with its stones numbered in chalk, because the yard was where things went that had come off a building. The Reader had assumed for a long time that it was the frame on the plate.

It was not. The yard frame folded in half perfectly: two equal jambs, the opening dead in the middle, a shallow bead moulding along the outer edge. A good frame, exactly symmetrical, and nothing to argue about.

The Reader put the two outlines side by side — the yard frame's, drawn by laying the real frame down on the card and tracing it — and the plate's.

Then they stopped, and went out, and got the yard frame itself, and turned it over.

On the back of the right-hand jamb there was a bead. Not a bead moulding. A bead of metal, run on and then ground back, about the length of a finger and the width of a thumbnail, sitting proud of the stone in a bead that had been filed nearly flush and never quite was.

The yard frame had two openings, and now it had one.

The Reader took the chisel and worked the bead out, and it took a long time. Underneath the stone was dressed — not chipped, dressed, with the same tool marks as the rest of the jamb — and the dressing ran inward, from the face into the opening, in the direction somebody works when they are standing inside a room.

Nobody seals a frame from the yard side. There is no way to get at the back of a jamb from outside; the whole purpose of a jamb is to be the part of the frame nobody touches. Both the north opening and the south opening on that wall had been sealed from the inside, by somebody in a room with no other exit, and the work had been done properly, and after it was done somebody had stayed.

The bead came out whole. The Reader held it for a while before putting it down, and only then noticed that it was warm, and then had to work out why it was warm.

There was nothing in that yard that gave off heat. The stone under their other hand had been cold the entire time they were working on it.
