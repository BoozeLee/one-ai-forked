# 06 — THE REACHER

The plate had a tower on it and no top.

The tower filled the whole of the upper frame and ran out through the edge, and there was no way of knowing how far up it went because the plate stopped before it did. Courses of stone, arches, a ledge that came out and went back in, a dark opening somewhere high on the left where a plate with light in it would have had one. Every scrap of drawing on the plate was spent on the tower.

At the bottom edge, on its own, one small hooded figure reached up toward it.

The Reader had a way of dating these plates. It was the field.

The Reader put a plate flat, put the ruler across it, and measured the length of the shadow the tower throws across the ground in the picture. The field on the plates is the same field — same horizon, same drainage cut, same two stones by the wall — so a tower in the same place throws a shadow of a length that means something. Eleven plates came in over a week. Eleven shadows. Eleven lengths, each shorter by very nearly the same amount, which is the sort of regularity that does not happen by accident and that a person leans on the way a person leans on a wall.

Not consciously. It had been eleven days of arithmetic.

They measured the eleventh against the first. A seventh of the field's width. A seventh less shadow meant a seventh nearer.

So the tower was nearer than on the first of the eleven. That held up to being measured again, and the Reader wrote it in the book with the date, put the plate back with the others, and stood away from the bench.

Then they went out to the field, because there was a thing they needed and it was not in the archive.

There was a ruin in the field. Nine courses high and no more, with a stub of arch on the east side and the field stones running out from its foot in a fan. They had walked past it two hundred times. It was the thing the field was for.

There was no tower on the field. There never had been. Nine courses, and above that a broken line and then sky.

The Reader stood in the middle of the fan of stones with the ruler and tried it anyway, which took four minutes and established that you cannot measure a shadow where there is no tower.

They came back in and looked at the plate.

The shadow on the plate was not cut off by the frame. It lay across the field and finished inside the picture, with the drainage cut and both stones visible along it, the way a drawn shadow finishes. But the tower did not finish. The tower went up and off, and the two things that ended in the same plate ended for different reasons, and the Reader had been measuring from the tower to the end of its own shadow.

Every one of the eleven had that in it. Every one had the tower running off the top and the shadow finishing inside. So the length they had been taking was the length of the shadow plus however much tower the plate left out.

And the more of the tower there got, the more the plate left out, and both of those went the same way.

Eleven days of arithmetic about the edge of a picture.

The Reader pulled the run back out and did it again properly. The courses, counting from the bottom up, which the plates allowed and the shadow did not: a course is a course, and a shadow is a thing somebody decided where to end. The first plate had nine. The eleventh had thirty-one.

Nine to thirty-one in eleven days, the figures moving by different amounts each time and agreeing with nothing at all, which is what a real thing looks like after eleven days. The shadow had agreed to a seventh. Nothing real agrees to a seventh.

So the tower was nearer. The measure had been wrong and the finding had been right.

They got to that at about four in the afternoon and then found, with some irritation, that they could argue themselves into eleven reasons a nearer tower was a good thing, and not one reason it was a bad one.

It could be climbed. It was being made, and a thing being made has a maker, and the archive had eleven years of plates without makers and no rule saying a tower could not. It was older than the oldest plate, because the ruin in the field was older than every plate in the drawer and the ruin was the tower's foot. The stone was the field's own stone, so it had come from nowhere, so nothing had been brought in, so nobody had come. It was nine courses high and harmless. It was nine courses high and thirty-one by Friday. It was on the far side of a wall from every road there was. And the figure at the bottom of the plate was reaching up, not away.

The one about the figure did the most work, and the Reader knew it did, and let it.

They took the lamp down low, put the plate on its side, and raked the light across it, which is what you do when you have stopped trusting what you are looking at.

The courses caught the light edge on. And they were not courses only. A rib ran up the left of the shaft and stopped at a ledge. Three stacks stood on the ledge, each one square-mouthed. A stair of rubble went up from the base of the first stack toward the right and left the frame.

The tower was not a picture of a tower. The tower was a set of instructions, drawn at a scale that only worked if you stood at the bottom of it.

The Reader put the lamp out and walked out to the field, because it was that time in the evening and the light was coming in low across the nine courses from the west, and the courses did the same thing they had done on the plate, and the rib came up out of the broken line, and the three stacks stood on the ledge with the sky behind them, and the rubble stair went up and to the right and stopped where the stone stopped.
