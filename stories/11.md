# 11 — FORGE OF VERTEBRAE

The plate had a spiral on it, made of nine turns of the same piece, each smaller than the one outside it, each nearer the centre than the one before, until the ninth was a tight curl no bigger than a thumbnail with a hole in the middle of it.

Each turn was a bar with a row of little round heads along one edge — some coral red, some sea green, two gold where the light fell. Where a gold bar crossed a green one the gold went over; where a green one crossed a coral one the green went over.

The ground was deep navy, almost black, with four-pointed stars scattered in it.

Eight of the nine turns were in colour.

The ninth was not. The tight curl at the centre was drawn in ink — line on bare paper, black, no wash, no colour inside it. The round heads along it were drawn, every one, and each was a ring and nothing else.

The Reader wrote down what they could see, because the plate would be measured later and the seeing was the only part that could not be redone.

Nine turns, tightening inward. Eight in colour, one in ink. Coral, green, gold, and the order they lay over each other: green over coral, gold over green, coral over gold. Three colours, three places, no fourth.

They numbered the turns down the side of the page, one to nine, outside in.

Then they read the sequence backwards.

That took the rest of the day, because backwards is not obvious from the front. From the outside in the turns get smaller, and in that order the plate is a thing winding in. Read from the centre out, and the first is the smallest with one pair of heads, the next one pair, each one after that one pair, and the sizes grow in a way a thing does not grow if it is winding in.

The Reader wrote the nine out flat in the book, one under the other: one, two, three.

They had expected the reverse to come apart. It did not. Each bar laid flat had a body in the middle and a wing off each side, the heads running in a row down each wing, and between the two wings a gap of the same width every time. Nine levels, nine pairs of wings, nine gaps the same width, and the gap running straight down the middle of the page the whole way.

It was not a spiral.

It was a column of nine vertebrae seen from behind, the small heads being what comes off the sides of each one, and the gap the place the cord goes.

They checked the heights next. Each level was the same height as the one below, all nine, top and bottom alike — wrong for a spine, and right for nine things drawn by somebody who was not drawing a spine.

Then they stopped looking at the plate and went and looked at the thing.

The Reader had no spine to hand and had not wanted one for some time.

They went out to the yard and under the step where the north wall's footing had come away, and in the gap there was the small dry litter of a thing that had lived in the wall. They took a claw and a few bones and laid them on the grid under the lens.

They put the plate against the bones.

The bones came apart at the joints. The plate's turn did not, which was expected and was not the finding.

The finding was in the order.

They took the lens to the ink curl and followed it out along the edge of the first coloured turn, and the coral went up to the ink and stopped at it, filled in right up against the line and not over it. Not once in nine plates was the ink broken by colour.

Then they went out along the outer turns, where the paint is thickest, and the colours lay over each other the whole way round.

And the ink line went under all of it, and came out the far side, and went on.

The Reader put the lamp down low and raked the plate and did it once more, because a thing found once is a thing found once.

One green head lay across the ink, two thirds round. It was the only place in nine plates where anything sat on top of the ink.

So the ink was not on top of the eight.

The ink was underneath them.

That was the whole of it. A plate is not one picture. A plate is a stack, each thing on it laid on the thing before, and the eight coloured turns were laid on the ink one. All nine were drawn. Eight were lit. The lit ones could have been drawn on anything, and they were laid on the one that had to be there first — the one nobody ever lit, still on the plate only because the eight on top of it stop short of the very middle.

And the lit ones were the ones that did the drawing.

The plate rendered the eight lit turns as light, with the ink underneath holding the spiral in shape the way the ground under a lit thing holds the light's shape.

Nine layers. Eight for seeing with. One for drawing on.

The Reader put the nine flat on the page again in the plate's order and read them as a column: nine levels, the gap the same width nine times, a row of small heads on each wing.

Then they turned the page over and read it from behind.

That is the only way anybody ever sees a spine. Not an animal's back, not anybody's front — from behind, with the long line of small points running straight down the middle, one to each level, which is why it is the shape that arrives when a person says spine.

On the back of the page the nine levels read the same way round. It does not matter which side of a spine you stand on for the part that counts.

The Reader squared the plate to the grid.

Eight coloured turns tightening inward, the ninth in ink at the centre with its row of heads and its hole, and under the low lamp the ink lying flat with nothing on top of it, and the hole the only part of the plate a thumb would go through.
