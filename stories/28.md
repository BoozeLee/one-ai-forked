# 28 — THE PLACEHOLDERS

The four hooks on the wall had been empty for as long as the Reader could remember, and they had long since stopped being hooks. They were marks. Somebody had measured them, cut them, squared them, and left them there, which is a different thing entirely from putting something on them.

The Reader counted them twice a day for most of a year, not out of duty but because counting was what the hands did while the rest of it worked. Two at the top corners, two at the bottom. Four thin lines running in from each, each line ending on a box that had been drawn with the same care as everything else in the frame and filled with nothing at all. The boxes were white. Everything around them was ink.

Nobody had forgotten to label them. That was the thing the Reader kept coming back to. Forgetfulness leaves no measurements. Forgetfulness leaves a gap where a wall is, and the wall is still a wall. These were gaps that had been surveyed.

Under the lamp the white of those boxes was brighter than the paper they sat on, which meant somebody had meant them. A blank that has been prepared is not an absence. It is a receipt.

So the Reader started looking at other blanks.

The ruins were full of them. That had always been true and had never before been a fact worth acting on, the way a floor is always covered in dust. Once the wall's boxes read as receipts, every unlit corner read the same way. Not empty. Unfinished. That difference was the whole difference.

They went along the outer wall first, where the stone gave way to older stone. A doorway opening onto a room with an empty shelf. A stair that climbed eleven steps to a landing leading nowhere, and the landing was squared, deliberate in a way no accident manages twice. A frame in the east wall where a rebate was cut and the fixings stood empty, threaded, waiting.

Each one was a page somebody had set up to write on.

The Reader took the lamp to the doorway shelf and stood there long enough to be sure. Dust, and under it four small circles in a rectangle, the ghost of feet that had once stood there. A thing had been on that shelf, the shape of a thing now gone, and the shelf remembered the weight.

They went back to the four boxes and looked again, properly, with the lamp turned so the white of the boxes caught it.

The boxes were not the same size.

It was a small difference. Barely enough to see without measuring, and the Reader did measure, and it was wrong by a fraction that meant nothing to a builder and everything to a person laying out a page. The top-left box was the largest. The top-right was smaller. The bottom-left smaller again. The bottom-right smallest of the four.

The same descent, in the same order, twice across, the way a size ramp descends.

The Reader sat on the bench and did nothing for a while. Then they took out the ledger — the eleven-year one, the ruled pages, the four blank ones at the front that had been blank since the day it was found — and held it flat on their knee, turned so the lamp fell evenly.

They were looking for a letter.

A glyph-shaped hole is not a shape stone arranges itself into. A blank in a wall is a blank in a wall. But a hole the exact height of a lowercase letter, the exact width of it, sitting where a letter would sit if the line above had run the full measure — that is a page set and then not printed. A font with one character missing.

And the four boxes were four instances of the same omission, at four sizes.

The Reader put a finger against the largest and reached for the next without lifting. The step down was consistent the way a ramp is consistent, the way a size list is — the way anything is when one decision was made once and then obeyed everywhere afterwards without anyone present for the obeying.

Something had been set in the ruins, with a name for most of itself and a hole for one part of it. The hole had been sized correctly. It had been sized correctly at four different sizes, which meant the sizing had been deliberate, which meant the omission was not an accident of the stone.

It was a decision, made by someone who intended to fill it, kept by someone who never came back.

The Reader closed the ledger on their knee and looked at the four badges — because that was what they were, badges, rounded rectangles with a hairline border and a shadow that made the emptiness sit up off the wall — and understood that the shadows were the tell.

A shadow needs something to cast it. Somebody had drawn the badges as though they were solid, had given them depth and a border and the small lie of a drop shadow, and then had put nothing inside them. The making was careful. The filling was absent. Both facts together made a third thing, which was the actual subject of the plate: not a missing label, but a label that had been withdrawn from something that had been finished without it.

Which was worse, and worth saying plainly, because the ruins were full of the other kind. A ruin never finished is honest: it shows where the work stopped and leaves the deciding to you — whether the masons were tired, out of stone, or interrupted. A ruin finished and then emptied is a different animal. It implies a second visit. Somebody came back, looked at the work, took the writing out of it, and went away.

The Reader stood in front of the largest badge for a while longer. Then they did the thing they had been avoiding since the count, which was to go and look at the four blank ruled pages at the front of the ledger, the ones that had been blank since the day the ledger was found, in a room on the other side of the ruins, by somebody who had clearly intended to fill them.

Four pages. Each blank. Each the same width as the last, each a little shorter than the one before.

Four pages, each blank, each a little shorter than the one before.
