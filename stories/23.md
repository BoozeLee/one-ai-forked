# 23 — THE ORNAMENT

The plate was full of ornament and the Reader was asked what it meant.

They went at it the way they went at everything now, and wrote it down first.

Crimson filigree on black, edge to edge with no bare ground in it. Magenta and purple in its shadow, gold laid over at intervals, and in the middle a wide oval medallion in teal and gold.

Everything on it was mirrored — left against right, top against bottom — so that any quarter of the plate taken alone was the whole plate again.

The Reader looked for what varies.

They looked for four days. They held it to the window, put the lens on it, and read from the outside in, looking for the place where the pattern broke.

It did not break anywhere.

Every scroll came back to where it started. Every curl went into the curl beside it and stopped. Nothing pointed at anything else, and nothing had been left out to make room.

They turned it a quarter turn. It was exactly as good that way up, which a plan is not and a map is not.

They wrote in the book: this plate carries no information whatever, and then they sat looking at the sentence for some time.

Every other plate in the drawer had something on it, and each one had given something up.

And this one was the best plate they had ever held.

The Reader wrote that down too, then wrote underneath it that they did not believe it, because every plate they had ever read had been about something.

So one of two things was true. Either this plate had failed and the machine had put out a beautiful thing with nothing behind it, or it had not failed and they had.

Or the plate had not failed and they had.

Then they went looking for something in this house that did nothing on purpose, because they needed to see one.

The ledger.

The ledger is a day book. One line a day for eleven years, in one hand, going back further in a different hand. Weather, who came, what was left of the light.

There are blank pages in it.

Near the front, four of them, ruled and empty, one after another and then not, as though the person filling the book had gone at it for four days and then stopped.

The Reader had read those pages for eleven years as gaps.

That was the reading they had: days not written down. Lost days, four in the middle of a run, when somebody had let the book go.

The ruling was still there, every line ruled the same as the lines either side, pressed in hard enough to have caught a nib, by somebody who meant to write on them.

Not one mark of ink on the four. No water, no thumb, no fold. Blank in the way a thing is blank when it was made ready and then left alone.

A ruled page in a book of one line a day is not a day not written down. It is a day kept.

The Reader put a hand flat on the page and understood the plate all at once.

A machine cannot do that.

They went back through the whole book — the plates, all twenty-two — and did the count of marks that do nothing.

Every mark on every plate in this drawer does something: a measurement, a report, a thing counted, a thing compared, a thing argued with. Not one mark in that square is not working.

And a machine does not waste. It is sent to do a thing and spends everything on that thing, and it has no way to put down a mark that goes nowhere, because going nowhere is not a thing it can tell.

Which means the plate that does nothing is the strongest evidence anybody has ever had that whatever made these is not a machine.

It is the only evidence there is. Everything else here could have been made by a machine asked carefully to make plates about towers and gates. The ornament could not. It is the part where the answer is not allowed to be a purpose.

The blank page is the same object seen from the other side.

A machine keeping a day book would put a line on every page, every day, whether or not there was anything to say. A ruled page left blank in a run of eleven years is the one instruction a machine fails, and it fails it four days running.

So there are two ways to see one thing and both are in this room: marks that go nowhere, and a page kept ready for marks that have not come.

Twenty-two plates, one useless, four blank pages. A bench that is not a bench, a room that is not a room, a light with an end on it, a gate four inches off the ground, all of it in service of the idea that things in here have to mean something.

It was a good idea. It had been the right idea for twenty-two plates.

But it had never once let the plates be beautiful for nothing.

The Reader did not write any of that down. Some of it is not for a book.

What they did instead was put the ledger out flat, open at the back, and rule the next page.

It took a moment. Ruling is a straight edge and a blade and does not care what goes on the page. They did two lines and stopped, because that was the shape of the four pages at the front.

And they left it.

And that was not an accident and not a job. One page in that book will not be day, put there on purpose at the end of a day they would remember, and it may be the only thing in eleven years of work done for no reason.

Then they put the ledger back and squared the plate to the edge of the grid and put the lamp where the lamp goes.

It was not going to be measured, or annotated, or in the book. There would be no second reading of it four days later, and nothing would ever be found out from it.

It is the only plate in the drawer that will never be written about again.

The ornament, left in place, doing nothing.
