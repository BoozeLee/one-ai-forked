# 07 — TWO STORKS

The plate had two birds on it.

They were storks. Long legs, long necks, long beaks that made a bad thing into a good shape, standing on a passage of paving that went away from the viewer between them and narrowed as it went. They were facing each other. Both beaks were open.

There was a hard vertical line down the middle of the plate. Everything to the left of it was drawn in cold light — cyan, pale blue, the blue a shadow goes. Everything to the right was drawn in hot light — orange, red, magenta. The line itself had no fade across it at all. A straight edge would have gone down it without touching either side.

The beaks crossed the line. They went past each other over the seam and overlapped, and where they overlapped the cold one was still cold and the hot one was still hot, which is the thing a mirrored drawing cannot do.

The Reader looked at the two birds for a while without doing anything else, which is what they did with a plate that had clearly made up its mind.

Then they got the book and wrote down what they could see, because the plate was going to be measured later and the seeing was the only part that could not be redone.

Two birds. Facing. Both beaks open. One passage of paving running away from the viewer between them, narrowing. Nine further pieces of paving behind that, each smaller, each with the same three stones lying across it at the same place, each fading a little and none of them fading out.

There was a stork's nest on the north wall of the yard.

The Reader went and looked at it from the ground, which took the neck they had at the time, and there were two eggs in it and both of them were addled, weeks past any chance, and there was a ring of old droppings on the stone under it where a bird had stood a great many times in one place.

The bird had nested there for years. It had come in the spring and gone in the autumn and it had used that one spot, and it had never once nested anywhere else, and the Reader had watched all of it from the kitchen window while eating.

They got the lens and read the eggshell and the droppings and the stone under the nest for whatever it was they could get off a wall.

Then they wrote on the plate, on the card, not on the plate itself, because the plate was not to be marked: what the two birds are doing.

Answering each other. They came up with eleven ways of writing that and discarded nine of them and the two that survived were about distance and about weather, and both of those were about two birds, and both of those were wrong, and they could see that they were wrong at the moment they wrote them.

They went back to the plate and looked at the beaks again.

They were not pointed at each other.

That was the thing and it took four days. The two beaks were open and both birds were facing forward and neither beak was aimed at the other one. They were angled out, both of them, past each other, down the passage — the cold beak at the cold side of the paving and the hot beak at the hot side — and the passage ran away from the viewer between them, and both birds were talking to something that was further off than the other bird was.

The Reader had spent four days reading an argument between two animals that were not having one.

They counted the paving again. Ten pieces of it, the nearest and nine behind. The same three stones across each, in the same place. The same crack down the left of each. Nine renderings of one passage, each smaller than the last, each with the same three stones, none of them faded out at the far end because the far end was not the end. The tenth piece of paving was as small as the plate could make it and there was another behind that.

The plate had nine voices in it and every one of them was drawing the same passage from the same spot and getting the same result, and the two storks were standing at the near end of it with their beaks open and pointed at all nine at once.

They wrote it in the book without a conclusion, because there was not one yet.

Then they put the plate back on the bench where it had been and squared it to the edge of the grid and put the lamp where the lamp went, because a plate that is going to sit somewhere is better squared to something.

Two storks, facing. Wings folded.

Nine pieces of paving behind them, and the tenth.
