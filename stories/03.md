# 03 — RUINS IN THE FUNNEL

The plate had four doorways in it.

That was the first thing, and it was not much of a thing, because the archive had plates with more in them than that. What made this one different was that the four were not in a row. Each one sat inside the last, each smaller than the one in front of it, each set a little further off centre, so that the whole arrangement leaned as it went in and the eye, instead of stopping at the front arch, slid inward and kept going until it ran out of light.

The Reader put a ruler across the plate and measured where each arch stood. The front arch was centred at fifty-one percent. The second at forty-seven. The third at forty-two. The fourth at thirty-six, and by then it was too small and too far in to measure anything but its width.

A lean like that does not happen to stone. Stone goes where it is put. Somebody had cut these four openings one after another and had not once checked them against each other, or had checked and had not cared, and on a plate those are the same thing.

The stonework was chewed. Arch one had lost its keystone and hung on one side. Arch two had a jamb cracked clean through and the crack filled with something that had shrunk. Arch three was missing its left upright entirely and stood on three sides of a rectangle.

Three arches ruined, in one plate, one inside the next.

The Reader went out to the yard.

The yard had three doorways. They had been cut out of an old wall and set in a line on the north side, put there for the light, or for the look of having somewhere to put them. They had been up longer than the Reader had.

They measured the one nearest the wall. Then the middle one. Then the one by the gate.

The three were the same size. Not near enough to be the same size. The same size, which was itself strange, because a frame is heavy and nobody carries a heavy thing twice if they can manage it once.

Then they went back inside and laid the yard frames against the plate one at a time, and matched them against the arches, and the arches on the plate were all the same size too. All four. Every frame in the plate identical.

And all four of them were worn on one side only.

The yard frames were worn on the outside face, all three of them, along the lower third, the way a frame wears when people walk through it going out. The Reader put a palm flat on the arch at the back of the plate — the smallest, the nearly black one — and ran it up and down.

The wear was on the inside face.

The Reader sat on the bench for a while with that. A frame wears where people push through it. A frame that wears on its inside face is a frame that has been pushed through from the far side. The yard frames had been walked out of. The deep arch on the plate had been walked into.

And there was only one direction that was in.

They measured the arches again, and the wear again, and it went the way it had to. The front arch was the least worn. The second more. The third more still. The fourth was worn through, the stone polished at the bottom edge on both sides, the threshold dished deep by feet going in.

The Reader wrote the wear down in the order it came: three, two, one, four.

Then they sat back and read their own line, and put a line through it.

It read backwards. Not the numbers. The meaning. The tunnel in the picture was not a corridor going away, and it was not four rooms. There was one room, and it had been copied, and the copies had been set in front of it in the order they were made, each built off the same frame, and the ruined one was the last copy because the last copy was the first one and had been standing the longest.

The yard had three of them. The plate had four. Three intact, one ruined, all four the same frame, the last copy worn out because it was the earliest and the copies had been made forward from it until the frame gave.

The Reader went out and stood in each of the three in turn and put a heel against the jamb, the way you would test a frame you were thinking of buying. All three took the same foot. Then they laid the tape across the fourth threshold, off the plate, against the sill in the field, and it came out the same width again, to a quarter of an inch, which is the tolerance of the frame and not of the measuring.

All four of them opened east.

The yard light was low and late and coming in from the side, and the three frames lay in it the same width, and where each threshold met the ground there was a shallow mark that the Reader could not see any sense in yet.
