# 14 — THE CHART OF EVERYTHING

The plate was covered in small squares.

Each square had a mark in it and a line of writing underneath.

The Reader held it at arm's length first, the way anybody holds something that looks like writing, and then brought it in close.

On the left the squares were about the width of a thumbnail. One mark in each, and a label under it.

Gate. Bench. Step. Wall, north. Wall, south. Drainpipe. The loose flagstone with the cut corner. The notch in the step.

Forty-one squares were still legible, and then there were not forty-two.

In the middle of the plate the squares were half that. Two marks to a square. Labels shortened to a stroke and a dot.

On the right there were no squares at all, only line, and the line thick and doubled, and where it thickened the shapes stopped being separate.

Under every legible label there was a second line, smaller.

The Reader brought the book and put it beside the plate.

The second lines were days.

Not the day the thing was made. The day it was last measured.

That is a different thing entirely, and the Reader had not been reading the chart as a chart at all.

They had been reading it as a list of what the yard is.

So they went out with the book and did it properly, item by item.

Gate, bench, step, north wall, south wall, drainpipe, the loose flagstone, the notch. Eight items, and eight squares, in yard order.

The plate was about the yard.

They were pleased about that, and wrote it in the book, and the word chart felt good to write.

Then they counted the squares again.

The yard had eight things in it they could name. The plate had forty-one legible squares, and the ones past the ninth were not in the yard.

There was no tenth thing.

The plate was not a chart of the yard. It was a chart that had the yard in it.

The eight they could check, and then thirty-three more, and then the right-hand third, where the labels had collided so thoroughly that two words sat on top of each other with no gap left anywhere to put a third.

The labels had collided and the chart could not be read.

That is what they wrote, and it was the first true thing in the book about this plate.

The Reader did not write down the second thing for two days.

The second thing was that they had read the right-hand third as damage.

As damp eating in. As a thing going. They had written it as the plate coming apart, and then dated that, and then believed it for thirty-one hours.

They had spent eleven years watching one thing in this house come apart slowly and they had trained themselves without noticing to read density as deterioration.

So the failure was theirs and not the plate's, and it was worth writing that plainly.

Then they took the lamp down and raked it and got the lens out.

The static on the right was not a thicker line.

It was the same line, the same weight, the same blue, drawn smaller.

Smaller all the way across. The squares did not stop. They came down to the size of a full stop, and then to the size of a dust speck, and they went on.

The forty-first was the last square with room in it for both a mark and a word.

Beyond that there is no size left on the page that will hold both, so the word goes over the mark, and the next word goes over that, and the chart does not collapse.

It goes to static.

And it is still all there. That is the part the Reader had not been able to see for thirty-one hours.

Everything past the forty-first is drawn as carefully as anything before it.

It is trying to hold everything at once, and the only thing that failed is the person standing in front of it.

The left third is simply the part drawn largest.

The yard is in it because a gate and a bench and a step and a wall are the largest things anybody ever measured carefully. There is nothing larger to spend the good part of the page on.

Everything after that is somebody being just as careful about something smaller.

Nobody chose to stop being able to read it.

It stopped being readable and then it kept going, which is the part that matters.

They found the last legible square and turned the lamp up.

It was the forty-first.

Under it, in the Reader's own hand, was a day. The last day anything in this house had been written down.

Everything to the right of the forty-first was later than the last time the book was used.

So the chart had not run out of things.

It had run out of people.

The Reader spent an hour on it after that, and it was the only hour of the week that went entirely.

They could get a word here and a letter there, the way you can pick out one conversation at the edge of a road with a dozen going. Each one cost more than it was worth and none of them joined up.

Sanity is a budget. Spend it and the page goes to static.

And that is what a budget is for. It is the thing that stops before the spending does, and it stopped.

After that they were not reading the plate. They were inside it, and the difference between those two things is the difference between the yard and the yard seen from the gate.

A chart of everything cannot be read by a person standing at arm's length in a room with one lamp.

It can be read by whoever is holding all of it.

So the plate was not unreadable. It was unreadable by them, and those are not the same sentence and one of them is worth having.

They put the book back on the shelf where it goes.

Then they squared the plate to the grid and put the lamp where the lamp goes, because a plate that is going to sit somewhere is better squared to something.

The left third legible. Gate, bench, step, wall.

And then the forty-first.

And then the line going on into a blue that was not quite solid and not quite clear.
