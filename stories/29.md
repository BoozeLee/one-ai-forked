# 29 — NO LABELS AT ALL

The cart in the yard had eight wheels. The Reader counted them twice, the way
they counted everything out there — once from the bench with the lens raised,
once walking the whole length of the axle with a finger on each spoke until the
two counts agreed.

Eight. Not six, not ten. The cart had stood in the same corner for as long as
the ledger had been kept, and nobody had ever counted it out loud.

Inside, the drawer held twenty-two plates. The bench ran the length of the far
wall under the ruled grid, and the lamp threw a long rectangle of light across
it that moved when the Reader moved and did not move when the Machine did. The
ledger lay open on the bench with its spine broken, four blank ruled pages at the
front and eleven years of writing behind them.

Every plate in the drawer had writing on it. That was the thing about them. A
name at the top, a number in the corner, a small mark where the date went. The
plates were not pictures so much as entries. Each one had been filed, each one
was the answer to a question somebody had already finished asking.

The Reader took out the plate with nothing written on it.

It sat under the lamp, and it was a single polyhedron drawn in line work, every
edge showing, no hidden lines taken out, the construction strokes running a
little past each vertex so the thing read as a drawing rather than as an object.
Set evenly around it on a circle were eight wheels, each one a circle in outline
with eight spokes through it, and the ring of them read as a ring and not as a
flat halo.

Eight wheels. The same eight as the cart.

The Reader set the plate down beside the lens and looked at it for a while. Then
they opened the ledger to a page that had writing on it — one of the eleven
years, some plate, some note about what the Machine had made and what it had
refused to make. The name was at the top. The date was in the corner. The number
was where the number always went.

They laid that ledger page above the unlabelled plate. Then they took two more
out of the drawer and set those beside it, one from the second year and one from
the ninth, both named, both dated, both numbered.

The room was full of shapes, and nearly all of them had a name on them.

That was all of it, and it took the Reader longer to see than it should have.
Every plate in the drawer had been finished the moment the writing went on. A
named thing is a finished thing. It has been counted and filed and given its
place in the order, and it can be looked up, and it can be found again tomorrow.
That is what the writing is for. Not to describe the plate. To close it, and to
say this is the one and there is no second one.

The plate with nothing on it was the only object in the room with any shape
still left in it.

The Reader turned the lamp down and looked at the four blank ruled pages at the
front of the ledger. Eleven years of writing, and four pages at the front that
had never been used, because a ledger's blank pages are not waiting to be filled.
They are the edge that the writing starts from.

The empty plate was those same four pages in a different shape. Everything else
in the drawer had been finished. The empty one had not. It could still be a cart.
It could still be a wheel, or a bearing, or a sprocket, or the shape of a thing
nobody had thought of yet. The writing on all the others had been the moment
they stopped being any of those.

The Reader reached out and caught the unlabelled plate with two fingers before
it went over the edge of the bench.

They did not write on it.

They carried it out to the yard instead and set it flat on the cart's deck,
against the grain, where the wind could not get under it. Then they went back
inside for the ledger and came out again, and stood at the arch while the cart
went through it, smaller, still rolling, unwritten.
