The plate had eight figures on it and all eight were the same.

Not similar. The same — same outline, same inner line, same small mark in the same place at the same height on each. They were eight copies of one thing standing in a ring.

The drawing was done in a single colour, a bright electric blue on a ground almost black, with no second colour anywhere on the plate. The archive is full of plates with two colours that had no business being two. This one had one thing to say.

In the middle of the ring was a disc, the brightest part of the plate, with rings inside it going in and at the very centre a shape the size of a fingernail.

The Reader put the lamp where the lamp goes and wrote down what they could see, because a plate is going to be measured later and the seeing is the part that cannot be redone.

Eight figures. One colour. A ring round a bright disc.

Then: inside each of the eight, a smaller one. The same shape again, and inside that another, and inside that a mark too small to be called a shape.

Eight figures, each with a cut-out of itself inside, going down the same path every time and never once arriving at anything different.

They read that much quickly and went outside.

There were eight chairs around the bench in the room.

The Reader had been in that room eleven years and had never counted them. They counted them now because the plate had a number in it.

Eight. Two had backs. Three had a split in the seat. One was the bad chair with the leg that would not sit true. Every one was a different chair.

The plate had drawn the same chair eight times.

That could be a plate about chairs. It was not, and the way it was not was worth an hour.

There was no gap anywhere in any of the plate's figures. They looked from the point of the lamp and again in raking light, and there was no opening in any of the eight — not at a hem, not between an arm and a side, not where two outlines should have parted.

They looked at the eight chairs properly, because the chairs were the only other thing in the building with eight in it.

A chair is mostly gap. Under the seat. Between the back rails. Between the legs and the wall behind. Eight chairs in that room, and daylight came through all eight in eight different ways. A drawing of one would have had to leave the light in.

The plate had drawn eight things with no light in them.

So the plate knew there were eight somethings in the room, and had drawn them shut.

The Reader read that word and sat with it, because shut is a strong word and they wanted to know whether they had earned it.

Then they went back to the edges, one figure at a time, with the lens, and did the thing that should have come first.

Every one of the eight was a closed outline. The line went round, came back to where it started, and met itself. All eight. Not one had a break in it anywhere.

Closed.

And here was where they had been wrong for two days.

They had been reading closed as sealed. Sealed means something is shut in. Closed means only that the line has stopped. A ring is closed, and that says nothing about what is behind the line or inside it.

The archive was full of closed things. So was the yard. A door is a closed line on a wall and a wall is a closed line on a field, and neither has ever been sealed by being closed.

They had looked at eight closed figures and read eight shut doors, and the plate was not the reason. The reason was that a person who has spent eleven years shut in with a plate hears a closed line as a locked one.

That went in the book, because it was the kind of thing that had to be put down before it could be looked at.

Then they counted the gaps.

Not openings. Gaps. Anywhere light could get through the shape, and a shape drawn on a plate is not a thing with an inside — it is a mark with an absence in it, and an absence is a place where the plate is not.

Eight figures, and each one had exactly one place where the plate was not.

Dead centre of each figure, a small round absence the size of a pin head, sitting in the middle of the smallest copy inside it — so the copies went down and down and the thing at the bottom of each was a hole.

Eight holes. One per figure. All the same size. All exactly in the middle.

The Reader stopped writing, got the plate, and held it up to the window, because one measurement was left and it was the only one that mattered.

The day was overcast and the window was the brightest thing in the building. They stood with the plate against it and looked.

The plate was opaque everywhere except in eight places.

The light came through in eight small shapes, each the shape of the figure it sat in, in miniature, and it was the colour of the ground and not the colour of the drawing.

So the eight figures had never been closed. There was nothing shut in them and nothing shut out. The plate was full of holes, and the drawing was only the thing that held the holes apart. A plate with eight holes in it needs eight figures because a figure is the shape a hole takes when you want to know where the hole went.

Closed is not the same as empty. It had taken two days to find that out, and the plate had been saying it the whole time in one colour and without a word.

They squared the plate to the grid and put the lamp back where the lamp goes, because a plate that is going to sit somewhere is better squared to something.

Eight figures in a ring, one colour, almost black behind them.

And eight small round gaps in the middle of them, with the window coming through all eight at once.
