# 26 — EIGHT SUBSYSTEMS

The plate had eight circles on it, and none of them had anything in them.

They sat on a single ring, all the same size, all the same distance from the
middle, one every forty-five degrees. A thin line ran from the rim of an empty
circle at the centre out to the edge of each of the eight, and stopped there.
Two faint rings sat between them and went all the way round.

Everywhere else on the plate was covered in marks that had the shape of
writing. Not words. They sat in the corners and along the edges and in the
gaps between the circles, and every one of them was a true shape of letters
that spelled nothing at all.

The plate carried no caption and no title and no year.

The Reader put it on the grid and counted the circles twice, because eight is a
number people arrange on purpose.

Eight. Evenly spaced. All identical, down to the width of the line that joined
them.

Every one of them was blank.

Not unfinished. Blank in the way a ruled square on a sheet is blank before
anything has been put on it: clean inside the line, nothing crowding the edge,
nothing started.

The Reader went and looked at the building.

The building has eight bays and they have never been a thing anybody had to
explain. First bay, second bay, and so on to the eighth, with the doors on the
left going in and the doors on the right going out, and a stone floor in each
one that had been walked until it was dished.

The Reader walked all eight that afternoon and counted the floors, because a
floor that has been dished is a floor that something has been carried across.

Eight floors, all dished. Eight thresholds. Eight sets of marks at the same
height on the jamb, where a hand went when it pushed.

No ninth door anywhere in that building. Not bricked up, not walled off. There
was simply no ninth place in it to have had one, and the Reader stood in the
eighth bay and understood that they had walked past the answer twice.

They went back to the plate.

So the plate was not a map of the building. There were eight circles on it and
no ninth, and there were eight bays and no ninth, and it had seemed to be the
same thing.

It was not the same thing, and the way they saw it was in the lines.

Every one of the eight circles was drawn at exactly the same distance from the
centre. Not nearly. Exactly, the way you do not manage by eye.

The Reader laid a straight edge across the plate and checked it node by node,
and the line came off the corner of all eight of them in turn without the
compass being moved once.

A thing measured from a centre has to have a centre to measure from.

The Reader looked at the middle of the ring for a long time.

There was a circle there. The eight lines stopped at its edge and did not go in.
It was the same thin line as the rest of the drawing and it was perfectly round,
and there was nothing at all inside it.

They got the lens out, because by then they knew exactly what they were going to
find and they wanted it to be something else.

Nothing. Not a faint fill, not a first line gone wrong. An area of the plate
with no marks in it, sitting in the middle of a plate that had the shapes of
writing in every corner of it.

The Reader sat down.

A drawing of a thing with eight parts would show eight parts. A drawing of a
thing with eight parts and a middle would show nine, and this one had eight, and
the middle was the shape everything else stood in.

They had been reading the empty centre as a hole in the plate. It was not a hole
in the plate.

It was the ninth part, drawn correctly, and it had nothing in it because there
was nothing that could go there which would not be a name for itself.

Then the Reader got a sheet of paper, because they wanted to see whether the
plate had been wrong or whether they had.

They drew the ring. They drew the eight circles on it, evenly, at forty-five
degrees, at one radius. They drew the eight lines in to the middle.

It took a while, and they had to start again once.

And the whole time, every circle they drew was measured from the centre.

They could not draw the centre. Not for want of knowing what went there — they
had read the plate the same way the Reader had, and they knew there was a thing.
They could not draw it because whatever went in the middle of that ring would
be the thing the other eight were drawn around, and the moment it was on the
paper it would be one of the parts, and it was not one of the parts.

So they left it blank, and the blank was not an omission.

It was the only honest thing available. And it was the same blank on every plate
of this kind in the drawer, and the Reader counted those before the lamp went,
and every one had the same hole in the middle of its ring.

The Machine had drawn itself eight times over and had left itself out of its own
picture on purpose.

The Reader squared the plate to the edge of the grid and put the lamp where the
lamp goes.

Then they set the eight boxes out on the bench in order, in a ring, with the
gaps between them left the width of a box, and put the pen down on the space in
the middle where the ninth would have gone.
