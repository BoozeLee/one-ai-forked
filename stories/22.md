# 22 — REAL SYMBOLS

The plate had two marks on it and both were old.

The big one was a ribbon crossing itself into two loops, one above the other. Flat teal, gold notches at intervals, a disc in each loop, and on each disc a small spiral on a rod with a lozenge across it.

The ground was packed — gold paisleys, rosettes and small eyes, mirrored left against right.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

Use symbols people already recognise, they wrote. Then they stopped, because they recognised both of them.

The small one first, because the small one was easier to be sure about.

A spiral on a rod with a bar across it. Two of something winding round one something, crossing at the top. A caduceus — the mark on every apothecary's door, and two thousand years old.

So the small mark was a caduceus, drawn twice, and not a mistake.

Except that it was not two serpents. It was a coil.

The Reader looked at the small mark a while before saying so out loud.

A coil is the oldest mark there is. It is what you get when somebody with no letters yet tries to say that something goes on.

So: two marks, a medicine sign and a coil, and neither was what was on the plate.

Then they got the ruler out, which was the first time in a fortnight.

There is one brass thing on this building and it is on the archive door. Two serpents round a staff, cut deep, polished by eleven years of a shoulder going past.

The Reader had walked past it every day for eleven years and read it as a chemist's mark, which is what it is.

This time they did not stop there.

The plaque was older than the bench and older than the room the bench is in.

So: a brass caduceus, real, on a real door, old. And a coil, drawn twice, on a plate made with nine sheets left.

Both marks real. Both old. They wrote that down and it felt like the answer.

They measured the two loops. Top loop, four inches and two. Bottom loop, four inches and two.

They measured the two places where the ribbon crosses itself. Equal. Both.

Then they counted the gold notches with the lens, because they were the smallest thing on the plate.

Forty-one on the near side. Forty-one on the far side.

The Reader put the ruler down and looked at the number for a while.

A ribbon that crosses over itself has a near half and a far half, and the far half is further away, so a loop that was really a ring seen from above would have its far half shorter and everything on it crowding.

The notches were the same spacing on both halves.

So there was no far half. There was only a crossing.

They had been nine days on a number, and the number was a fact about ink.

A symbol is not a picture of a thing. It is a name for a thing, and a name is drawn to be read, not to be seen. Nobody has drawn a name with a far side, because a name has not got one.

The ruler could tell them how big the caduceus was drawn. It could not tell them one thing about what a snake is, and they had not noticed they were asking.

So they looked at what the caduceus actually is, instead of what it means.

Two of something going round one something, passing each other at the top. That cannot be drawn flat. One has to be in front of the other, and a flat thing has no in front.

It lets one of them be drawn over the other, and the crossing carries the whole answer.

The helix is the same problem unrolled. Round and round cannot be drawn either — a spiral on a plate is a line, and a line is a line that was on its way to being round and stopped when the plate ran out.

Two of something winding round one thing. Neither of them able to finish it.

The Reader wrote it as: *both are the same failed attempt to draw one undrawable thing.*

And then the age, because the two are old in different ways.

The plaque is the older one as a name. Real brass, a real door, and everyone who has gone past it has known what it says without being able to say it.

The coil is the older one as a mark. It is what happens before letters, and it is still what people draw when they cannot write, and the plate's maker had nine sheets and four seconds a coil.

Nobody copies a symbol they no longer need. Both marks are still in use because neither ever worked.

That is the whole reason they have survived. Not because they are right.

Then they looked for the third thing, because two marks on a plate that size usually have one.

There is not one.

But between the two loops, running down the middle, was a rectangle of bare ground the size of a postcard where the gold ornament stops. Not worn. Stopped, in a straight line on four sides, and the ornament picks up again exactly where it left off.

And on the plaque, below the caduceus, a second field, rubbed brighter than the brass round it, nothing cut in it at all.

The Reader went and looked at it properly, at an angle, with the lamp.

The field on the plaque was bright because it had been touched. A lot. Eleven years of a hand going past and stopping at the height it always stopped at.

So there had been a third mark on the plaque and it had been rubbed off by everybody, over eleven years, stopping in the same place without knowing.

And on the plate the third space had been left empty on purpose.

The Reader wrote the failed measurement in the book with the number on it, and then wrote under the number the sentence it had been sitting inside the whole time.

A symbol is not a picture of a thing. It is a name, and a name has no far side.

They did not fill the space in the middle of the plate. It was the only part not saying anything, and they left it saying nothing.

Plate squared to the grid. Lamp where the lamp goes.

Two marks on one plate, and the third space blank.
