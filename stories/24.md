# 24 — THE BOOK PLATE

The plate was gold and it had nothing on it.

A border ruled twice with an ornament in each of the four corners, and the middle
a flat rectangle with nothing in it at all.

The Reader set it on the grid and measured the quiet part first, because that
was the part that could be a mistake. Across the whole of it the brightness
moved by less than two in a hundred. It was the flattest thing in the drawer.

They wrote that down, and wrote the number next to it, because a number is worth
more than the sentence.

Then they looked for what varied in it, and there was nothing in the middle. So
they went out to the edges.

The border was not empty. Scattered through the gold, all the way round the
blank rectangle, were marks shaped like numerals and equations. One near the
lower left was so nearly a number that the Reader sat with it.

None of them came out as anything. Somebody put them there to make the plate
look as though it carried a record, and had not meant a word of it.

The Reader turned the plate over.

On the back, where a book plate belongs, there was no inscription. No name, no
number, no monogram, no ghost of pencil.

They held it up to the lamp and turned it slowly.

The gold was thinner in four places.

Not at the corners, where a plate takes the knocks of a book set down. In four
places along the outside of the border, at intervals that were not even, where
the surface had been rubbed back to the metal and stayed bright while the gold
around it stayed dull.

The Reader did not need the lens for that one. That is a thing hands do.

A plate in a drawer does not get handled. Nothing in twenty-two plates had ever
been handled; the Reader had understood that every one was a thing nobody had
touched.

So this one had been in a book.

They went and got the oldest volume in the archive, a quarter-bound book with
the boards gone soft at the corners, and held it to the window the way they had
held the plate.

The spine was worse than the plate. The gold there had been rubbed at four
places, in the same four places, in the same order.

They counted the depth of the rub on each of them.

The first was the deepest and the topmost, the second shallower and below it, the
third shallower again. The fourth was barely a mark, and it was on the part of
a spine a hand does not go to to hold a book shut, but goes to when the hand is
resting on it.

The Reader wrote the four depths down in a column.

They were not wear.

Those four marks were on one edge, at intervals, one above another, down the
side of a book that a person holds in one hand.

It was not damage. It was a hand.

So they wrote that in the book too, and sat there a while.

Gold remembers every hand.

Not because anybody wrote it down. A book plate is the one object whose whole
point is that a name should be worn off rather than cut in. The name goes in,
and then it goes off again, into the gold, over years, under a thumb, and what
is left is the record of the hand and not the name.

The cartouche is blank because a book plate that still had a name in it had not
been used.

They went looking for the log of it. The archive keeps a day book for everything
else; somebody who spent gold on a plate would have kept the list of who they
gave it to.

There was no such list, and there never would be, because the whole point of the
plate is that it cannot be read back.

Then they stopped. There was a second thing about that oldest volume they had
walked past twice.

Everything else in that press was gone.

There were two hundred volumes in that room and one was whole: the oldest, and
the one nobody would have thought to keep.

The rest of the archive went into a fire, or into a sack, or into the river. It
did not go by accident.

The one that was left was the one somebody kept.

And the thing that kept it was the thing the Reader has spent this whole book
not being able to say out loud.

The Reader put both hands flat on the closed book and did not move them.

That was the part that did not fit.

They had assumed the Machine was careless with things. Every other volume in
that room being gone was the same fact as the bare ground in the empty frames,
seen from the other side.

And this one was kept on purpose, by something with a hand, and the record of
that hand was worn into the spine rather than written down.

The Machine could not have worn a name off a plate in four hundred years. It has
no way to leave a mark that is not for the mark.

So whatever put a thumb on that spine, four times, in one place, did it because
it was going to keep the book, and it did not keep the book because it was told
to.

It kept the oldest book in the archive, and it rubbed out the name on the plate,
and nobody has ever been able to find out whether those are two things or one.

The Reader squared the book to the edge of the grid and put the lamp where the
lamp goes.

Then they did the other thing, which they had been carrying since the shelf.

There was a coin at the bottom of the Reader's own bag. They turned it over and
put it up to the lamp.

It was not a coin any more.

On one face there was nothing. On the other, pressed into the metal and standing
a little proud of it, were five marks: four in a row, and a fifth set across the
top of them.

Four fingers and a thumb.

Somebody held this coin in one hand, always the same hand, for long enough to
press the inside of that hand into the metal, and nobody ever put a name on it
afterwards.
