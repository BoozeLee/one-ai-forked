# 21 — CHAPTER ONE

The plate was crowded.

There were figures all over it, standing in a spread that went from one edge of the square to the other, and between them there was almost nothing: the ground close and flat and black, and one blue rectangle hanging in the air with lines ruled across it.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

Two skulls, it said. A moon with a mark in it. A small figure in a hat with one hand up. A bare-armed figure holding a square thing and not looking at it. A rope of bones going from the middle to a sun.

And then a line under the list, which is where their own thinking usually went.

A cast mid-action is a scene. A cast posed is a poster. This is a scene.

They were pleased with that. It went in the book dated, and they left it four days, which was longer than usual.

The archive keeps a count.

It has kept it for eleven years in a hand older than the bench. Every plate has a number against it, how many figures were on it, and under the number, how many plates came before.

They had never been asked what the count was for and had never gone and looked at what it added up to.

So they went and looked at it.

Plate one had no plate before it. Under the number was a dash.

Every other plate had a figure under the dash, and none of them was the same twice.

They sat with the book a while, then went back to the crowded plate and counted the figures on it again.

There were six that were doing something. Two skulls and everything else was ground, sun, rope, moon, foliage and glyphs, none of which is anybody.

Six.

They wrote six in the margin, then did the thing they had been avoiding, which was to ask what six was doing.

Everything on that plate was still in the middle of its own business.

The hand in the hat was up and still coming. The square thing was being carried and not looked at, which is what a carried thing looks like when the carrying is the whole of it. The rope of bones was going, and where it went was off the side. The bare-armed figure had the thing in both hands and had not begun whatever it was going to do with it.

Not one had arrived anywhere. Not one had sat down, or put anything down, or turned to look at any of the others.

So the reading was right. It was a scene and not a poster. Everybody was still in it.

They wrote that down too, and meant it, and then read the counts again, because a number that agrees with you is not a number that has told you anything.

Plate two had seven figures and one plate before it.

Plate three had four figures and two plates before it.

The counts went down.

They walked the whole list. The figures got fewer as the plates got older: nine on plate nine, two each on the two after, one figure on the last, eleven plates before it and nothing on it but a hand.

The book was not counting a cast. The book was counting a cast leaving.

They sat back from it.

Nothing on this plate had finished, because nothing on it could have. Zero plates came before it. And on a first page there is no such thing as caught in the middle, because nothing has happened yet for anybody to be in the middle of.

They had it backwards, and could not see it for four days, and the backwardsness was the good part.

The plate was not holding a moment. It was a beginning that had not been added to. Every figure was at the first second of whatever it was, and the plate did not know either.

Not a scene caught in the act. A scene before it starts, which is the only kind a person can be sure of.

They went through the whole book again with the second reading and it held everywhere.

Plate one: six figures, none of them anywhere. Plate nine: two figures, looking at a thing neither has picked up. Plate eighteen: one hand, holding nothing, in the middle of an empty square.

Every plate in the drawer is earlier than the thing that happens in it. Not one of them has the next part on it.

Which means the count in the archive's hand was never counting anybody arriving. It was counting who was still standing.

The Reader closed the book and left it where it was.

Then they went back to the plate with one question left and got the answer in a minute, which was a mercy after four days.

Direction.

Six figures, and every one was facing forward or facing into the picture. The one in the hat had the face turned with the raised hand, away from the viewer and into the light. The bare-armed one was square to the front. The rope went right and out.

Not one of them had its back to the bench.

The Reader thought about that for longer than they expected to.

The back of a person is only visible to somebody standing behind them. A person walking towards you shows you their face; a person standing still shows you whatever they last decided to show. The back is a thing that only happens to a figure once something has already gone past it.

And on the first page nothing has gone past anything.

There is nobody in this picture to see the back of. Not because a scene cannot have somebody turned round in it, but because this scene has not had one thing happen in it yet, and until something has, everybody is going forward.

Four days ago they had written that it was a scene because everybody was still in it, and that had been correct, and it had been correct for the wrong reason.

They put it in the book with both reasons, the wrong one first, so the page would show the working.

Then they squared the plate to the grid and put the lamp where the lamp goes.

The first page, and none of them turned round.
