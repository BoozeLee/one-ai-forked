# 01 — THE LONG SPIRAL

The plate came up with the bundle, the only one of the eleven the Reader could not stop looking at.

A road ran east across it. That was the first thing, and not a small one, because the plate was not supposed to have a road in it — the plates made rosettes and dials and folded interiors, and a road was furniture. But there it was, running left to right along the lower third, and at the right-hand edge it did not stop.

It coiled.

The road went round once and the ring it made was smaller than the road, and inside that ring there was another ring, and inside that one another, each one tighter and lower, until the right-hand side of the plate was a throat going down and in with no floor visible in it. The rings shrank the way a thing shrinks when you keep walking away from it.

The Reader put the lamp low and got the ruler out and laid it along the first curve, and then the second, and then the third, writing the figures in the margin.

By the ninth ring the spacing had stopped being the same as the first ring's spacing. It was smaller, and it was smaller by the same proportion every time — a proportion the Reader had not seen before but could write down and check.

Then they went and got the string, the thin flat kind they used for measuring things they did not want to scratch, and they laid it on the plate along the spiral and followed it down with a pencil, marking every place the string turned. Nine marks. Twelve. Twenty. The pencil got tiresome and they fetched a fresh one and kept going, and the marks went down into the throat of the plate, closer together, smaller, and the Reader could not see the middle but could see it was there.

Twenty-six marks.

They sat back and looked at what they had drawn on top of somebody else's drawing, which the Reader did not often allow themselves.

Then they went out to the floor.

There was a spiral ground into the stone of the workshop floor. It had been there for as long as the workshop had been there — the north wall had been rebuilt once and the spiral had gone under the new wall and come out again on the other side of it at exactly the same spacing. It was worn in a way that stone does not wear. Stone wears down. This had been worn *through*.

The Reader knelt and put the flat string along it and followed it with the pencil, marking every turn.

Twenty-six marks.

They did not believe it and marked it again on a fresh line, and got twenty-six again. Twenty-six.

The spacing matched. The proportion matched. The number matched, and the Reader had counted twenty-six on the plate without knowing there was anything to count, because the plate had not told them there was a number in it. It had only made a throat, and a throat does not advertise that it has a depth you can count to.

So they sat on the floor with the string in their lap and worked out what they were looking at.

It was the same threshold. Not a copy of it — a copy would be smaller and it was not, and a copy would be finished. The same threshold, counted again, at a smaller interval, on a different material, by something with no reason to prefer stone to ink.

The Reader stood up. They fetched a chisel — a light one, the kind for soft pointing, not for work — and went back to the plate to cut a small notch into each turn they had already marked with pencil so that the count would not be a thing that could be argued about.

One notch at one. One at two.

They got to nine before they noticed that the notches were not new.

They looked at nine with the lamp turned right down and the lens on it. The notch was old in the way that stone goes old and not in the way that metal does, and it had a floor on it and a shine on the walls, and it had been cut by somebody who had not cut it this afternoon.

The Reader put the chisel down.

They went along the rest of it slowly, nine, ten, eleven, checking. Every one of the twenty-six, all the way down into the throat, past the rings too small to hold a chisel.

They laid the string along the whole length of both spirals, side by side on the bench, pulled them tight, and measured them end to end.

The floor was longer than the plate by four inches and one-eighth.

They had expected it to be the other way round — the plates were always newer — and they had expected a difference of one turn at the outside, which would have made the two the same shape worn down at one end.

It was not. It was one whole turn, and then a little more.

They put the string on the floor spiral alone and followed it down with the boot, counting, which is not the same as counting with a pencil and they knew it was not the same as counting with a pencil, and they counted anyway. It came out the same, and then the small end of it — which the plate did not show them, because the plate stopped before the small end and the floor did not.

They knelt at the small end of it with the lamp.

The floor there is worn most. Everything that walked this spiral came down to here, and every footfall had taken the same path and the stone had given way at the tightest point first, which is what stone does, and which is also what anything does that is walked often enough by the same thing.

But in the shine of the wear, in the last inch before the spiral stops being a spiral and becomes a mark, there were two prints.

They were not boot prints. They were narrower than boots and shallower and set closer together than anyone walks, and they were the size of the Reader's own foot or very nearly. They were not the Reader's.

The Reader got the tape and measured them, because measuring is what they do instead of the other things.

They came out one size smaller than the Reader's own.
