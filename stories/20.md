# 20 — ONE REAL THING

The check was not the Reader's idea, and it had never been the archive's either.

It came out of a failure, three years back: a plate of a bird that the archive had logged as *passerine, unidentified*, and which had taken eleven months to write off. Eleven months. A plumage arrangement that no ornithologist in the building could place, produced without effort, in the middle of a night, and worth nothing. The Reader had stood over it for a week with the plate under glass and understood that the plate was not a mistake. A mistake is rare. This was fluent.

So the Reader began checking.

That was the whole of it. No instrument, no schedule. In the long window of the bench room there was a yard, and in the yard whatever the season had left standing, and once a week the Reader went down and looked at a real thing and then came back up and put it next to a plate. Eye. Hand. Note. Eye, hand, note. They had done it every week for three years, and the tally on the inside of the cabinet door was a column of marks in pencil, one for each subject, one additional mark for each failure.

The column was long.

The One drew beautifully. That was never in question and never needed to be. The birds had the right number of joints in the wrong order. The flowers had the right idea of a flower and the wrong idea of gravity, which is a sentence that sounds like nothing until you have seen a plate of a heavy thing floating. The trees had bark that read as bark at arm's length and resolved, under a lens, into a repeating motif of about nine strokes, applied with the same confidence in every direction, which is not a thing bark does.

A rose, though.

It arrived in the autumn bundle, plate forty-one, and it was a rose. Greyscale, which was the register. Dead centre on a grid that receded to a low horizon, which was the composition every plate in the archive used and which the Reader had long since stopped finding remarkable.

They checked it because they checked everything.

They brought it up from the yard first, a stem with three blooms gone over and one still holding, and they laid it beside the plate, and they looked.

The plate had fourteen outer petals and nine inner. They counted. The live flower had fourteen and nine. The plate's inner petals were folded back the way inner petals fold, tight at the base and opening along the seam, and the ones behind them were darker because they were deeper, and the ones at the shadow side were darker still because the light came from over the Reader's own left shoulder, which was where the plate's light came from, and which had been the position of the lamp in the room the plate was made in, four rooms away and a year and a half back.

They got the lens.

The thorn count was right. Two, low on the stem, on opposite sides, one of them old and blunt and one of them thin enough to catch. The leaf count was right, and the leaf was compound rather than simple, and the little leaflets along it were attached the way leaflets attach, which is not the way a person draws them when they is inventing a leaf. The plate was not an idea of a rose. It was a rose, at a resolution nobody had seen one at, done by something that had been in the same room as a real stem and had not been asked to make anything.

The Reader sat back.

They did the arithmetic, because they always did the arithmetic, and it was worse than a failure. Failure was cheap — a bird that could not be placed was a bird that cost a week and taught nothing. This was the opposite. Every other subject in three years of notes had come out of the One the way a dream comes out of a person: internally consistent, gorgeous, and made of nothing.

This was a report.

And the Reader understood, sitting at the bench with a lens and a dead stem and a plate of a rose, that there had to be a difference somewhere — a switch, a mode, some state in which the One stopped inventing and started copying — because a thing cannot do both with the same apparatus without something deciding, and if something was deciding then there was more than one thing.

They went and got the oldest plates out, and the ones from the first year, and laid them in a row along the wall, and looked for the reports.

There were two more. A length of moss. A particular fracture pattern in a slate tile from the north wall of the yard, which was not a crack anyone would design, which was a crack that had happened to that tile in that order. Three reports in nine years, out of several hundred plates, and the three were all of things the One had been close to in the sense that mattered: a rose, a moss, a slate. Not abstractions of them. Not rose-shaped. The actual local instances.

The Reader stood up and went to the cabinet and got a fresh sheet of the ruled annotation stock and made a column on it, and at the top of the column wrote the word they had been trained out of using, the archive's word, the one that implied a craftsman with a plan and a slow accumulation of skill across a long life.

They crossed it out.

Then they wrote, underneath: *it is not remembering us. It is not inventing them either. Two different jobs.*

And they put the plate of the rose back under glass, in the place on the shelf reserved for the ones that had passed, which was the second shelf, and which had been empty for a very long time.

Under the lens, on the fourth outer petal, near the rim, there was a single hairline defect where the ink had pulled away from the tooth of the plate. The live flower had the same kind of damage on the same petal, and the plate had copied it, and the copy was the only untrue thing about it.
