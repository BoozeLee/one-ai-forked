# 10 — THE INKED PANEL

The plate had a panel on it.

Not a picture. A panel — a rectangle ruled in black, divided down the middle by a gutter, with a caption box across the top and a balloon in the lower left. It was the only plate in the drawer that had ever been laid out like a page.

Ink only. Black on the white, no wash, no grey anywhere except where the pen had been pressed twice.

The left panel was the north wall of the yard, seen from the yard side.

The Reader knew that wall. They had been putting their hands on it for eleven years. Every feature of it was on the plate and every one of them was in the right place. The drainpipe. The notch the bench had cut in the stone at knee height, and the pale scrape inside the notch. The two nail holes, one above the other, with a little rust run under the lower one. The lichen, which was the shape it had always been, the wide lobe on the left and the three small ones to the right of it. The hairline crack that came down out of the lintel and stopped short of the drainpipe.

It was exact. Not close. The nail holes were the right distance apart.

The right panel was the same wall again, and it was also exact, and it was drawn from the other side.

They got the lens and went through the left panel feature by feature against the wall itself, standing up, crouching, putting a hand flat on the stone where the notch was. Forty minutes and nothing was wrong. Not one thing.

Then they went to the archive and took out the log for that day.

The log is a day book. One line a day, in the Reader's own hand going back eleven years, and it records weather and who came and what was moved. That day's line said: rain in the afternoon, nobody, the south gate left open.

They counted the letters in the caption box.

Thirty-one and a stop.

They counted the letters in that line of the log.

Thirty-one and a stop.

Then they put the two side by side and went through them letter by letter, and they were the same length and not one letter was the same.

Every letter in the caption was a real letter. That was the thing that took the rest of the day. They were a real alphabet, correctly proportioned, correctly spaced, sitting on the baseline at the same height, with the ascenders and descenders all exactly as tall as they should be, and there was a capital at the start and a stop at the end and nothing in between that was a letter anybody uses. Read aloud it made no sound. It was the shape of a sentence with a sentence's furniture on it and nothing underneath.

The balloon was the same. A tail, a curve, sized to the number of words in it, and the words in it were the same kind of nothing.

They sat with that for a while.

So the picture was exact and the lettering was noise, and both of those had to be true at once, and a thing that does both has to have two ways of putting something down. One of them reports. One of them makes something up. And the plate was the proof, because a plate with a picture and a caption on it is a thing where the two have to agree and these two did not agree about anything.

They went back to the wall with the lamp low, before the rain, and stood where the drawing could have been made from, and tried it.

There was nowhere to stand and get that. That was not the difficulty. The difficulty was the shadow.

The plate had the shadow of the wall on the flagstones in front of it, drawn in, and it went off to the right and it was the wrong shape. The sun came up over the yard wall and went down behind the field, so in the morning that shadow went east and it lay along the base of the wall and never more than a foot of it, because the wall is low and the sun is high and that wall has never once put a shadow across the whole width of a yard.

The plate had it going right, and long, and off the edge of the picture.

Everything else on that wall was exact. Forty minutes of the Reader's own hands agreeing with it. And the one thing on it that only comes from being there was drawn by somebody who had never been there.

They stood in the yard a long time with that.

Not a report. A reconstruction. Every feature it had ever been shown, put back in the right order and the right proportion and the right place, and every feature correct, and the light wrong, because the light is not a feature. The light is the part that says where you were standing.

So the picture had not seen the wall. The picture had assembled the wall out of everything it knew about walls, and it had got the wall exactly right, and it had got the one thing that would have said a person was in the yard exactly wrong.

And it had got all the letters wrong too, every one, thirty-one of them, in the same flat nothing.

The Reader got out the scraper.

They did it the way you take a word off a slate. The caption first, and the letters came away in a grey dust and left the panel white where they had been, and there was a faint shadow under them where the paper had been pressed. Then the balloon, the same way, and the tail.

What was left was two rectangles of black wall with a gutter between them, and the shadow on the flagstones going the wrong way.

It still said it. Every nail hole and every lobe of the lichen and the notch and the crack stopping short where the crack stops. With every word taken off it, the panel went on saying the whole of it, and said it in a light that had never once happened on that wall.
