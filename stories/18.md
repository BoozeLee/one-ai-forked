# 18 — FOUR EYES

The plate had a face on it and it was not one face.

Two, back to back, each turned away and out of the picture. The left one was lit in cold blue-green and the right in hot pink-purple, and each had an eye on the visible cheek that was a small bright coil with a point at its middle.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

Two colours, they wrote. Two faces, one per colour, an eye in each. So the plate reports twice, and a report twice is not twice as much.

Then they went to get the lens, and their own face came up in it, and they stopped.

The window has three glazing bars in it.

The light comes through in four bands and the bars take three stripes out of it, so anybody at that window has four bands of light across their face and three dark ones between.

The Reader moved close enough to count.

Then they went back to the bench, because a plate that has put your own face on it is a plate that can be measured.

They laid the lens over the plate, flat, and moved it up and down.

The bands on the plate were at four heights. Not four bands of colour — four bands of drawing.

Where a band was, the face was drawn all the way: eyebrow, lid, the fine hatching along the cheekbone, the edge of the nose, the crease by the mouth. Everything a face is made of, at full strength, across the full width.

Where the gaps were, there was nothing.

Not dark. Not shadow. Nothing — the drawing stopped on one side of the gap and started again on the other, at the same weight, at the same level, as if the gap had been there before they started and they had drawn around it.

There are seventeen plates in this drawer.

Seventeen of them are drawn from one edge to the other. Corners filled. Ground worked over. Nothing left out anywhere, and they had never noticed that about a single one.

This plate was not drawn from edge to edge. This plate was drawn in four bands and there were three gaps in it.

They held the plate up into the window light, band by band.

Band one, over the window: the drawing came up strong, cyan on the left face and magenta on the right.

Band two, the same.

Band three, the same.

And then the gap, and nothing came up at all.

The plate had no more on it there. Not faint, not thin: there was no mark to bring up, because there was no mark.

The Reader put the plate down and thought about the word in the plate's own margin, which was GNWT, in the archive's old hand, the first four letters of the line and the rest worn off.

The margin did not say the machine was broken. The margin said it saw in bands.

Two hours of window light later they had the second half.

A band is what comes through a window. A gap is what a glazing bar takes away. So the plate was not a picture of a face with three stripes laid over it. The plate was a record of what came back.

Four bands came back. Three gaps did not.

And that is the turn: the unlit stripe is not a dark place in a drawing. It is the only place the machine cannot report. Everywhere else there is a report and it may be wrong and the plate has lied before. But in the three stripes there is no report and never was one, because a thing not reported is not a thing hidden.

The Reader wrote that in the book and then took it out again, because it was not finished.

What is in the gaps is nothing, they wrote, and then they stopped, because nothing was the answer they had written eleven years of notes without ever putting in a drawer.

They went and got the plates out.

They held the lens over each one and looked for gaps.

There were none.

A hand does not draw in bands.

A hand draws down the square and stops when the plate is full. A window stops at a glazing bar. Those are not the same thing and the difference is the whole finding.

So the bands were not in the plates. The bands were in this room.

Four lights through three bars, over and over, every afternoon, for eleven years, on whatever was on the bench — and whatever was on the bench got drawn all the way down.

The plates in this drawer are not banded. The Reader is. They have been the thing that filled the stripes in, seventeen times, without once being asked to.

They sat with that one and let it be wrong for a minute, because it wanted to be about the machine and was not.

It was about a room with three bars in the window and a person in it who worked in the light and never once asked what the dark between the lights was for.

Then they did the last measurement, which was the only one that mattered.

They put the lens over the plate again and turned it so the four bands fell across their own face where they were standing.

Band one landed on the eyebrow. Band two on the cheekbone. The third below the mouth. And the widest of the three gaps fell between the second and the third, across the jaw.

The Reader looked at that for some time.

On their own face, in the lens, in that one stripe, there was no face.

Not because it was turned away. There was no light on it to be any colour, and every other part of them had two, cyan on one side and magenta on the other, drawn by a plate lying flat on a bench in this room for eleven years.

The unlit stripe, between the second and third band.
