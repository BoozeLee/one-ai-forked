# 05 — RORSCHACH

The plate had a chair on it.

That was unusual enough to be interesting on its own. The archive ran to plates of dials, of wings, of four doorways in a row, of a rose, of things with a seam down the middle of them, and the Reader had a fair idea of what this archive thought a chair was for, which was nothing.

This one had a plain chair. Four legs. A flat seat. A plain back, no splat, no carving, no upholstery. The kind of chair that exists because somebody had to make a chair.

And the plate had the chair twice.

There was a hard vertical line down the middle of the plate, and the chair was on both sides of it. Left of the line, a chair. Right of the line, a chair. The two were the same height, the same depth, the same four legs, the same flat seat, and they were not the same chair.

The Reader got the ruler out and checked, because this was the kind of thing that had turned out to be nothing more than a drawing being uneven, and they had been wrong about that before. Seat depth, both sides: the same. Leg spacing: the same to a sixteenth. Seat height: the same.

Then they looked at the front legs.

The front leg on the left of the line went down and slightly forward. The front leg on the right of the line went down and slightly back.

There is no way to build a chair with a front leg going backwards. The Reader had built a chair. It had been a bad chair and it had taken them a Saturday, and the one thing a Saturday teaches is that the front legs are where the load goes and the load goes forward.

So the plate had not failed to draw a chair. It had drawn two chairs, both of them correct, and had set them side by side.

The ink-work behind them mirrored too. Every cross-hatch on the left had a twin on the right running the same way, and if the Reader let their eyes go soft and looked at the whole thing instead of the parts, it resolved into a shape that was almost a face, and then stopped resolving.

They had a chair in the room. It was the only chair in the room and it was not a good chair either. They brought it to the bench, put it on the plate, moved it about, and held it against the left half and then the right.

The chair in the room was three decades of bad joinery and a seat planed flat by use. It was not the plate's chair. The plate's chair was a chair that had never been sat in, and the plate's two chairs disagreed about which way forward was, and neither of them was the room's chair.

Which was fair. The plate had no business drawing the room's chair. The plate drew what it drew.

They put the chair back and went to get the lens, and the bloom was already there, at the lower right, in a corner where ink had a habit of sitting when a plate had been kept in a damp room for a year.

Blooms are a property of the plate. The Reader had pulled eleven of them out of the archive over the years and every one was the same soft round creep with a hard edge where it stopped, and not one of them looked like anything.

They looked at this one.

This one had a weight to the top of it, and two places where the ink had pushed out further than the rest, one above the other, and the two were the right distance apart for the eyes of a person. Every person alive has that exact width.

The Reader sat back.

Not theirs. That came out of the plate without effort, the way the hood came out of the plates in the drawer: not a failure, not a blur, drawn with the same care as everything around it.

So it was a face, and it was not the Reader's, and the plate had drawn it near and large and flat on.

They went and got the margin note.

*Faces that far away and no nearer.* In their own hand, in this room, a fortnight ago, about a plate with a whole figure on it standing off in the distance so small that at arm's length it was a smudge and had to be brought closer to be seen at all.

They went through the archive with that note and a loupe, and it took four days. Faces on plates: nine. All of them small. All of them at the far end of whatever they were standing in — on a road, at the end of a corridor, across the back of a room. None near. None filling anything.

The plate had the rule and the plate had the exception, and the exception was two eyes' worth of ink in the corner of a plate about a chair.

The Reader put the plate down under the lamp, and the ink was wet enough to move when the plate was tilted, and it was still spreading.

They slid a sheet under it and tilted it a little and watched, and the bloom crept out another two millimetres in about a minute and then stopped, and where it stopped it had two more places in it, one above the other, the right distance apart.

The Reader wrote the count in the margin under the old note, in the same hand, and then left the plate flat on the bench with the lamp at its usual angle and did not move it again.

The bloom settled where it had stopped: two eyes' worth, a centimetre and a half across, and a hard edge where it had decided to stop.
