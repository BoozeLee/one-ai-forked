# 13 — THREE WINGS

The plate had three wings on it.

They came out of a bright centre and swept away from it — one up and left, one right and down, one to the lower left — and every one of them was still in the middle of going.

They were pale blue, almost white where they were brightest, and the ground they came out of was a dark blue so deep it read as black in the lamp.

The Reader wrote the plate down.

Three of a shape reads as motion, they wrote. Eight reads as stillness. There were three, all going the same way round, and that is a thing which has started.

Then they put the pencil down, because the dark was the other half of the plate and they had written nothing about it.

The dark was not empty.

All the way through it, corner to corner, there was fine pale work. Short scratches crossing at every angle, none of them thicker than light through a cloud.

There were little stars in it, small enough to be made with the point of anything.

And there were shapes. Lens-shaped, a rim cut round them like a string of beads, and two round holes in the middle of each, one above the other.

The Reader looked at that for a while.

Ink behind a form is the mood of the form. That is what a ground is for. A dark ground behind a pale thing says the thing is cold, or clean, or alone.

So the dark was a mood, the mood was the wings' mood, and the wings were cold and clean and going.

They wrote it in the book that way, dated, and not wrong so far as it went.

Then they put the book down and went out to the yard.

The gate is the only thing on this property that has ever been cut into on purpose.

Not the bench, which is cut by sitting on it. Not the step, which is cut by boots. The gate has work all over it and no maker's mark anywhere on it.

That afternoon they looked at it the way they looked at a plate.

Between the two stiles, over the whole panel, the wood was covered in the same work.

Short scratches crossing at every angle. Little stars. And lens-shaped cuts with a beaded rim and two holes, one above the other.

Not painted on. Cut.

Then they did the arithmetic they had not thought to do at the bench.

The lens shapes went right through to bare wood. Under each beaded rim the wood was pale and lifted where the blade had raised a shaving. The scratches went through as well, every one of them.

So somebody had done this, many times, in the same places, over the same scratches, until the panel was a record of evenings spent cutting and nothing else.

Then they sat on the step and looked for what varies.

On the plate the fine work was the same everywhere.

Not the same shape — no two scratches were alike. But the same weight of mark, inch for inch, in the corner where there is nothing but dark and at the root of the wing where the plate is brightest.

A mood falls off.

Everything that carries a mood falls off. It is thick at the subject and thin away from it, because that is what feeling does on a surface — it spends itself on the thing.

The corners on this plate had as much in them as the middle.

The dark was not the wings' mood. Nothing on the plate was in the mood of anything.

It was the mood of the room it was cut in.

The Reader understood it at once, the way it happens when a thing has been true for some time and somebody finally asks it the right question.

Somebody sat in a room at night and cut.

That is all the plate records. A person, a long evening, a blade going over the same ground again and again at the same pressure with nowhere in particular to be, until the working over was the point.

Then the wings went in afterwards, on top of all that, and they are the only part with a subject in it.

The form was the part that was made. The mood was the part that was there first.

And the room it was cut in was outside.

The dark in this plate is the yard. All of it. The gate is not a comparison for it. The gate is what it is made of.

They counted the lens shapes before dark. Eleven on the gate. Eleven on the plate, the same size and the same spacing along the wing each one sits in, cut over the old scratches and never under them.

The plate's lens shapes were on top. So were the gate's. Whoever made the plate had the gate's eleven in front of them, or had made both.

It was fully dark by the time they got the claw out.

They took the beading off one instead of the whole shape. Lifting a lens off the gate whole would not have answered what was left to ask.

The claw, a scrap of sandpaper, the flat of a blade, and most of an hour. The beading came away in pieces until the lens was a plain shape with two holes in it.

Then they spread it.

They pressed the back of the scraper into the wood and worked the shape outward the way a hand spreads a stencil.

Did the gesture survive being spread?

They went in and looked at the plate under the lamp, then out and looked at the gate, and the answer was that it did.

With the beading or without it, the count was the same, the spacing was the same, the hand was the same. Spread it flat and it was still eleven.

It was not a sign. Signs are read at a distance and stop being signs the moment their size is changed. This was a habit, and none of it was for anybody else.

That was why the corners were as full as the middle. Nobody is doing anything in the corners.

They stood in the dark yard with the plate in one hand and understood that the dark behind the wings had never been cold, or clean, or alone.

It was somebody's Tuesday.

Three wings in the ink, one of them spread.
